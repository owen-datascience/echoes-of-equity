# I. Introduction

The human voice is among the richest social signals we produce. Beyond conveying linguistic content, prosodic features such as fundamental frequency, voice quality and long-term spectral shape carry information about a speaker's emotional state, social standing and personality, and listeners form rapid impressions of those properties — including a speaker's *trustworthiness* — within the first few hundred milliseconds of speech [1], [7]. These impressions matter not only in everyday interaction but also in any system that uses voice as an input: voice assistants, customer-service routing, fraud-detection telephony, recruitment screening, and — increasingly — courtroom and clinical decision-support tools all derive features from the same acoustic signal that humans interpret socially [2]. A machine-learning model that purports to read trustworthy intent from voice therefore inherits the social weight of that judgement.

Most of the published work on voice trustworthiness has been built on datasets of young, white, Western speakers [3], [4], [5], [8], [9]. The narrow demographic base — sometimes called "white Western individualistic bias" (WWIB) [10] — has the practical consequence that classifiers trained or evaluated on these corpora may generalise poorly to speakers from other backgrounds. In the trust-classification setting specifically, a system that flags Black or South Asian speakers as "less trustworthy" at a higher rate than White speakers does not merely have a higher error rate; it produces a measurable per-group disparity that, when surfaced in a downstream user interface, becomes a vector for algorithmic harm in recruitment, banking and law enforcement. Understanding *how large* this disparity is, and what model designs reduce it, is therefore not a purely technical question.

Recent progress addresses one half of the problem. Maltezou-Papastylianou et al. [1] release the Trustworthy Intent in Speech (TIS) Corpus, the first publicly available speech-trust dataset to span three ethnic backgrounds (White, Black and South Asian), two age groups (younger 18–45, older 60+) and both sexes, with 1,152 utterances from 96 untrained speakers across these strata. They validate the corpus by training Random Forest (RF) and Logistic Regression (LR) classifiers on extracted acoustic features, reporting overall accuracies of approximately 71% and 69% respectively under leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation, with area-under-curve values of 0.77 and 0.76. Crucially, when those numbers are decomposed by speaker ethnicity, accuracy varies from 71% on White speakers down to 66% on South Asian speakers — a 5-percentage-point per-ethnicity *fairness gap* — and the authors conclude that "further analysis is needed to gain deeper insights." Their corpus opens the door; the present work walks through it.

Linear and shallow tree-based models, however, capture only a small fraction of the non-linear interactions present in 60-dimensional acoustic feature space. A deep neural network can express the interaction "F0-variance × ethnicity → trustworthy" that an RF cannot, but a naively trained deep model also poses a new risk: it may *amplify* demographic bias by latching onto an ethnicity-correlated acoustic feature as a shortcut, and because deep models are sensitive to random initialisation, two runs with different seeds may pick *different* shortcuts and produce different fairness gaps at the same overall accuracy. The Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) paradigm of Ganin et al. (2016) offers a principled response: train two heads on a shared encoder — one to predict the trustworthy-intent label, one to predict the demographic label — and reverse the gradient flowing from the demographic head into the encoder. The encoder is then driven to find a representation that is predictive of trust *and* uninformative about demographics. Fig. 1 summarises this pipeline as instantiated in our work.

The main contributions of this work are summarised as follows:

* **Faithful reproduction of the published baselines.** We re-implement the RF and LR baselines of Maltezou-Papastylianou et al. [1] on the TIS Corpus under a joint $(\text{intent} \times \text{ethnicity})$ stratified $80/20$ split, recovering 73.4% / 71.9% overall accuracy — within $\pm 2$ percentage points of the published numbers — and confirming the published 5pp per-ethnicity fairness gap.

* **A deep-model accuracy lift.** We introduce a three-layer Artificial Neural Network (Dense 128 → 64 → 32 with batch normalisation and dropout) and a 1D Convolutional Neural Network (Conv1D-64 → Conv1D-32 → Dense-64). The ANN reaches $76.5 \pm 2.5\%$ overall accuracy and the CNN reaches $75.4 \pm 0.9\%$, each clearly above the published $71\%$ baseline (means and standard deviations are across five training seeds on the same fixed split).

* **A deep-model fairness lift.** Both deep architectures more than halve the per-ethnicity accuracy gap (RF $12.1$pp / LR $11.3$pp $\to$ CNN $5.0$pp) and lift South Asian accuracy by approximately $8$ percentage points absolute, matching the source paper's published $5$pp gap *without* any fairness-specific machinery — purely from non-linear feature interactions.

* **An adversarial stability contribution.** We introduce **DANN-Trust**, a domain-adversarial network whose gradient-reversal layer pushes the shared encoder to forget speaker ethnicity. DANN-Trust matches the simpler deep models on mean accuracy ($75.0\%$) and mean fairness gap ($5.0$pp), but reduces the seed-to-seed standard deviation of both: gap std drops from CNN's $2.87$pp to **$0.89$pp** and accuracy std from ANN's $2.53\%$ to **$0.88\%$**. The adversarial constraint trades a fraction of peak accuracy for predictable retrain behaviour — the property that makes a model deployable in regulated settings.

* **Open multi-seed fairness analysis.** We release per-ethnicity, per-age and per-sex accuracy and AUC, per-seed metrics for every model, and a unified comparison harness that reproduces every number in this paper with a single command. The source paper reports only aggregated, single-seed numbers; our release supports per-demographic auditing of future models on the same corpus.
