# II. Related Work

This section reviews three areas of prior work that frame our contribution. Section II-A surveys the acoustic correlates of perceived trustworthiness that motivate every classifier in this paper. Section II-B summarises the documented evidence of demographic bias in voice-based AI systems and the under-studied state of bias in *voice-trust* classification specifically. Section II-C introduces adversarial debiasing — the methodological family from which DANN-Trust is drawn — and observes that no published voice-trust system has applied it.

## II-A Voice Acoustics of Trustworthiness

Listeners infer personality traits, including trustworthiness, from very brief voice samples (McAleer, Todorov, Belin, 2014; Ponsot, Burred, Belin, Aucouturier, 2018), and these inferences are remarkably consistent across listeners. The acoustic correlates that drive trust judgements have been studied in detail. Lower fundamental frequency (F0) is associated with perceived competence and leadership in both male and female voices (Klofstad, Anderson, Peters, 2012; Tsantani, Belin, Paterson, McAleer, 2016), while higher F0 variability (pitch range and standard deviation) is associated with warmth and engagement (Schirmer, Chiu, Lo, Feng, Penney, 2020). Harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR) reflects the periodicity of vocal-fold vibration: a higher HNR indicates a cleaner, more harmonic signal and is typically read as a healthy and authoritative voice (Ferrand, 2002), while a lower HNR suggests breathiness or vocal strain. Jitter and shimmer — micro-fluctuations in pitch and amplitude, respectively (Felippe, Grillo, Grechi, 2006) — index vocal stability; small variations correlate with vocal control, while large variations correlate with vocal disorder and, in trust studies, with lower perceived reliability. Cepstral peak prominence (CPP) is a robust measure of voice quality; a low CPP indicates a breathy voice (Hammarberg, Fritzell, Gaufin, Sundberg, Wedin, 1980; Jalali-najafabadi, Gadepalli, Jarchi, Cheetham, 2021). Long-term average spectrum (LTAS) summarises the resonance profile across an utterance and is sensitive to vocal-tract length, which in turn correlates with body size and the dominance impression that often accompanies male voices (Da Silva, Master, Andreoni, Pontes, Ramos, 2011; Puts, Hodges, Cárdenas, Gaulin, 2007). Maltezou-Papastylianou et al. [1] consolidate these six feature families into a single acoustic feature set for trustworthy-intent classification, which we adopt verbatim in Section III-B. Their Random Forest Gini analysis identifies F0 mean, F0 SD, HNR, shimmer and CPP as the dominant contributors, with LTAS contributing little; our Section V-G replicates this ranking.

## II-B Demographic Bias in Voice AI

The above acoustic literature is built almost entirely on young, white, Western speakers (Baus, McAleer, Marcoux, Belin, Costa, 2019; Oleszkiewicz, Pisanski, Lachowicz-Tabaczek, Sorokowska, 2017). Taylor and Rommelfanger (2022) coined the term *white Western individualistic bias* (WWIB) to describe the resulting narrowing of generalisable findings in neuroscience and psychology; the same critique applies to voice-AI corpora. The practical consequence of WWIB-trained models is now well-documented in adjacent tasks. Koenecke et al. (2020), examining commercial automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems from Amazon, Apple, Google, IBM and Microsoft, report word-error rates approximately twice as high on Black speakers as on White speakers across all five vendors. Tatman (2017) similarly documents systematic gender and dialect disparities in YouTube's automatic-captioning pipeline. Sap, Card, Gabriel, Choi and Smith (2019) extend the finding from acoustic-level bias to text-level bias in downstream hate-speech classifiers, demonstrating that bias compounds across pipeline stages.

By comparison, demographic bias in voice-based *trust* classification is markedly less studied. The TIS Corpus [1] is the first publicly available speech-trust dataset to span three ethnic backgrounds, and the source paper's per-ethnicity Random Forest results (White 71%, Black 68%, South Asian 66%) are, to our knowledge, the only quantitative measurement of per-group accuracy disparity for this task. Our work extends that single data-point into a five-model, five-seed comparison and is the first to apply adversarial debiasing techniques to this corpus.

## II-C Adversarial Debiasing

The Domain-Adversarial Neural Network (DANN) of Ganin, Ustinova, Ajakan, Germain, Larochelle, Laviolette, Marchand and Lempitsky (2016) was introduced for unsupervised domain adaptation: train a feature encoder so that its representation is *predictive* of the task label while *uninformative* about which domain (e.g., source vs. target dataset) the input came from. The mechanism is a single layer — the Gradient Reversal Layer — that acts as the identity in the forward pass and multiplies the upstream gradient by $-\lambda$ in the backward pass. The encoder is thereby trained against an adversarial domain classifier and converges to representations that the adversary cannot decode.

Zhang, Lemoine and Mitchell (2018), at the ACM/AAAI Conference on AI, Ethics and Society, adapted the same mechanism from domain adaptation to *fairness* by replacing the "domain" label with a *protected attribute* (race, gender, etc.) and reframing the goal as demographic parity rather than cross-domain generalisation. They demonstrated the approach on lending and income-prediction tasks. Wadsworth, Vera and Piech (2018) applied an analogous adversarial fairness setup to recidivism prediction on the COMPAS dataset. Subsequent work (Madras, Creager, Pitassi, Zemel, 2018; Beutel, Chen, Zhao, Chi, 2017) has extended the adversarial-fairness framework to broader notions of fairness including equal-opportunity and counterfactual fairness, and to multi-task settings with multiple protected attributes simultaneously.

To our knowledge no published work has applied adversarial debiasing to voice-based trustworthy-intent classification, or to any task on the TIS Corpus. The closest prior work is in *speech emotion recognition*, where Meng, Yan, Yan, Liu, Wu and Zheng (2019) use a domain-adversarial encoder to remove speaker-specific (rather than demographic) characteristics from the emotional representation. Our DANN-Trust extends this idea by treating speaker *ethnicity* — rather than speaker *identity* — as the nuisance variable, and is the first to quantify the variance-reduction property of adversarial debiasing across random initialisations, an aspect of DANN behaviour that the original literature does not measure.
